# C1 Advanced: exam structure and marking

A factual reference to the shape of the Cambridge C1 Advanced examination, and
to how the two papers this application covers are marked.

**This is not a Cambridge publication and has no official status.** It was
written for this project. The figures come from `src/cae/domain/parts.py`, which
is the specification the application itself generates and marks against, so what
the coach says here and what the marker does cannot disagree. It contains no text
reproduced from Cambridge's own materials: no sample papers, no candidate
answers, and no assessment scale band descriptors. For the exact wording of the
band descriptors, consult Cambridge's published handbook directly.

Detail is given for Reading and Use of English and for Writing, which are the two
papers this application covers. Listening and Speaking are summarised only.

## The exam at a glance

### Papers, timings and weighting

C1 Advanced has four papers, and each contributes a quarter of the final result.

- **Reading and Use of English** — 1 hour 30 minutes, 8 parts, 56 questions,
  78 marks.
- **Writing** — 1 hour 30 minutes, 2 tasks, each marked out of 20.
- **Listening** — approximately 40 minutes, 4 parts, 30 questions.
- **Speaking** — about 15 minutes, 4 parts, normally taken in pairs with two
  examiners.

The whole examination runs to roughly four hours. It is offered both on paper and
on screen; the tasks are identical in both formats and only the way answers are
entered differs.

### How results are reported

Results are reported on the Cambridge English Scale. Each paper produces its own
scale score, and the average of the four is the overall score. Every candidate
receives a Statement of Results showing the overall score, the grade, and a
separate score for each paper, so a weakness in one skill is visible rather than
hidden inside an average.

A score of 180 is the pass mark and corresponds to grade C. Higher grades are
awarded above that, and a candidate who performs below C1 but within range may
receive a certificate at B2 instead. Scores are placed on the scale by
statistical equating between sessions, which is why no published table converts
raw marks to scale scores: the conversion changes from one session to the next.

## Reading and Use of English

### Overview of the paper

Eight parts, 56 questions, 78 marks, 1 hour 30 minutes. The paper is a single
timed block, so time spent on one part is taken from another.

Parts 1 to 4 are the Use of English half and test control of vocabulary and
grammar directly. Parts 5 to 8 are the Reading half and test comprehension of
longer texts.

Parts 1, 2, 3 and 8 award one mark per question. Parts 4, 5, 6 and 7 award two,
which means the second half of the paper carries more weight per item than its
question count suggests.

Texts are drawn from a wide range of authentic sources — journalism, non-fiction,
academic writing, and in Part 5 sometimes fiction. They are edited but not
simplified, so an unknown word is expected and should be worked around rather
than solved.

A workable division of the 90 minutes is roughly eight minutes each for Parts 1
to 3, fourteen for Part 4, sixteen for Part 5, fourteen for Part 6, sixteen for
Part 7 and fourteen for Part 8. That leaves a few minutes to transfer and check
answers.

### Part 1: Multiple-choice cloze

**Format.** A text of about 150 to 180 words with eight gaps. Each gap has four
options, labelled A to D, and one of them is correct. 8 questions, 1 mark each, 8 marks in total.

**What it tests.** Vocabulary, almost entirely. Some items turn on collocation or
a fixed phrase; others turn on the difference in meaning between words that a
dictionary would list as near-synonyms. A few test which preposition or
complementation pattern a word takes. Pure grammar is not tested here.

**How to approach it.** Read the whole text before answering anything, because
some gaps depend on information further on. Then, for each gap, decide what the
sentence needs before looking at the options — the four choices are designed so
that more than one looks plausible in isolation, and only one is right in this
particular context.

The distractors are chosen to be tempting. They will usually be the same part of
speech and the same register as the answer, and often related in meaning. What
separates them is what they combine with. When two options both seem to mean the
right thing, the question is which one is actually said.

### Part 2: Open cloze

**Format.** A text of about 150 to 180 words with eight gaps and no options.
Candidates supply the missing word themselves. 8 questions, 1 mark each, 8 marks in total.

**What it tests.** Grammar and structure, with some lexico-grammar. The answers
are function words — articles, auxiliaries, prepositions, pronouns, quantifiers,
relatives, linkers — or a word that completes a fixed expression or a phrasal
verb. A word that could only be guessed from meaning alone would not be a fair
item, so the answer is almost always structural.

**How to approach it.** The answer is always exactly one word. An answer of two
words scores nothing, however sensible. Contractions are not usable.

Some gaps can be filled from the immediate phrase, but others depend on the
sentence or the paragraph, so it is worth reading past the gap before deciding.
Where more than one word genuinely fits, the mark scheme allows the alternatives.

Common triggers at this level include inversion after a negative adverbial, cleft
sentences, comparative and concessive structures, relative clauses with
prepositions, and parts of fixed phrases.

### Part 3: Word formation

**Format.** A text of about 150 to 180 words with eight gaps. Beside each gap a
stem word is given in capitals, and the answer is a different word built from
that stem. 8 questions, 1 mark each, 8 marks in total.

**What it tests.** Word formation: prefixes, suffixes, internal spelling changes
and compounding. Understanding of the surrounding text is needed too, since the
gap has to be filled with the right class of word.

**How to approach it.** Work out the word class first from what surrounds the
gap. After an article or an adjective a noun is needed; before a noun, an
adjective; modifying a verb or an adjective, an adverb.

Then check three things that cost marks routinely. Whether the sense is negative,
because there is usually at least one gap in each task requiring a negative
prefix. Whether the word needs to be plural, since a correctly formed singular
noun in a plural context scores nothing. And whether an internal spelling change
is involved, as in long to length or high to height.

Spelling must be correct for the mark. Both British and American spellings are
accepted, but not a mixture within one word.

### Part 4: Key word transformations

**Format.** Six separate items with no passage. Each gives a complete lead-in
sentence, a key word in capitals, and a second sentence with only its beginning
and end supplied. The gap must be filled so the second sentence means the same as
the first. 6 questions, 2 marks each, 12 marks in total.

**What it tests.** Grammar and vocabulary together, and specifically the ability
to say the same thing a different way. The structures tested range across the
passive, causatives, conditionals and hypothetical forms, inversion, modals of
speculation, reporting structures, phrasal verbs and fixed expressions.

**How to approach it.** Three rules decide most of the marks. The answer must be
between three and six words inclusive, counting the key word. The key word must
appear exactly as given, with no change of form whatsoever. And contractions
count as two words, so *didn't* uses two of the six.

The two sentences must carry the same meaning, with nothing added and nothing
lost. An answer that is grammatical but strengthens or weakens the claim is
wrong.

**Marking is in halves.** Each item is split into two parts worth one mark each,
so a partly correct answer can score one out of two rather than nothing.

### Part 5: Multiple choice

**Format.** One long text of roughly 700 to 800 words, followed by six four-option
multiple-choice questions in the same order as the information appears. 6 questions, 2 marks each, 12 marks in total.

**What it tests.** Detailed comprehension. Items ask about the writer's attitude
and opinion, the purpose behind a paragraph, the main idea, what is implied
rather than stated, the tone, and what a word or phrase means where it stands.
Organisational features are tested too — how the text exemplifies, compares and
refers back. A recurring demand is telling apart viewpoints that are similar but
not identical.

**How to approach it.** Read the text once for the overall shape and the writer's
stance, then work question by question. For each one, find and mark the stretch of
text that answers it *before* looking at the options, then choose the option
closest to what that stretch actually says.

The most common failure is deciding from a glance at the text rather than from
close reading of the relevant lines. Every correct answer is provable from the
text; if a choice cannot be pointed at, it is a guess.

Distractors are wrong for nameable reasons: true but not answering the question
asked, true of a different part of the text, a reasonable inference the text does
not support, or a partial truth that overstates.

### Part 6: Cross-text multiple matching

**Format.** Four short texts on a single related theme, by four different
writers, followed by four questions. Each question asks which writer shares or
differs from another named writer's view on a specified point. 4 questions, 2 marks each, 8 marks in total.

**What it tests.** Comparison of opinion and attitude across texts. The question
names the subject of the opinion but not the opinion itself — identifying what
each writer actually thinks is the candidate's job.

**How to approach it.** Read all four texts first and work out each writer's
position on each point under discussion, in your own words, before touching the
questions. The task is comparing positions, not matching sentences.

The texts have an academic flavour without demanding subject knowledge, so
practice with dense noun phrases and academic vocabulary helps. Writers typically
agree on some points and differ on others, so a writer who agrees with another
about one thing may disagree about the next.

### Part 7: Gapped text

**Format.** One long text of roughly 500 to 600 words with six paragraphs
removed. Seven paragraphs are supplied, labelled A to G: six fill the gaps and
one fits nowhere. 6 questions, 2 marks each, 12 marks in total.

**What it tests.** Text structure, cohesion, coherence and global meaning —
whether a candidate can follow how a text is built, not merely what it says.

**How to approach it.** Read the whole gapped text first to get the structure and
the development of the argument. Working gap by gap without that overview is the
usual cause of wrong answers.

For each gap, check both directions. Most wrong answers come from picking a
paragraph that reads well after the preceding text without also testing whether
what comes next still joins up.

Placement is proved by cohesion, not by topic: pronouns and determiners with
nothing yet to refer to, time references, linkers that imply what came before,
lexical chains, and definite articles introducing something already mentioned.
Each paragraph is used once at most. Do the certain ones first and eliminate.

The unused paragraph is deliberately on-topic and plausible; it fails because it
contradicts something, repeats information already given, or breaks a reference
chain.

### Part 8: Multiple matching

**Format.** A single page of text, either continuous, or divided into sections,
or made up of several short texts, with ten questions. Each question states
information found in one place in the text. 10 questions, 1 mark each, 10 marks in total.

**What it tests.** Locating specific information, detail, opinion and attitude by
scanning. The questions are printed before the text, which is deliberate: they
tell the candidate what to look for.

**How to approach it.** Skim and scan rather than reading every word, and
practise doing it against the clock — this part carries ten questions and is
easily the most time-pressured.

Scan for the idea, not the wording. Questions paraphrase what the text says, so
matching vocabulary is a poor guide and often a trap. Sections are written to be
comparable, so similar-sounding information appears in more than one place;
having found a topic match, check that the specific claim in the question is what
that section actually asserts.

Some sections answer more than one question. Pay attention to qualifying words in
the question — *first*, *only*, *despite* — since a section frequently matches the
topic but not the qualifier.

## Writing

### Overview of the paper

Two tasks in 1 hour 30 minutes. Part 1 is compulsory; Part 2 offers a choice of
three. Both require 220 to 260 words, and each is marked out of 20.

The two tasks carry equal weight, so a strong Part 1 cannot compensate for an
unattempted Part 2. About 45 minutes each, including planning and checking, is
the natural division.

**Length is part of the task.** Writing significantly under the range usually
means a required element has been left out. Writing significantly over it usually
brings irrelevance, repetition or weak organisation. Neither is penalised by a
word-count rule directly; both show up in the marks.

### Part 1: The compulsory essay

**Format.** Always an essay. The input gives a context — a class discussion, a
talk, a programme — together with three points and, usually, some opinions voiced
about them. Register is formal to neutral-academic; the reader is typically a
tutor.

**What the task requires.** Discuss **exactly two** of the three points, and say
which of the two is more important, with reasons. This is where Content marks are
most often lost.

Discussing all three thinly is treated as an omission, not as extra effort.
Discussing only one is a serious omission. And an essay that covers two points
without ever judging between them has not completed the task, however well
written it is.

The supplied opinions may be drawn on, but lifting them unchanged does not count
as writing: the rubric asks candidates to put things in their own wording wherever
they can, and that instruction is part of what is being assessed.

**Structure that works.** An introduction framing the question, one paragraph per
chosen point, and a conclusion that states and justifies the comparison. A
position stated in the introduction and returned to at the end reads as an
argument rather than a summary.

### Part 2: Choice of three tasks

**Format.** Three questions, of which one is answered, in 220 to 260 words. The
genres used are the letter or email, the proposal, the report and the review.
Each task names a context and a reader, and both determine what a good answer
looks like.

**Choosing.** Pick the task whose genre conventions you know and whose content
you can fill with something specific. A familiar genre answered with vague
content scores worse than a less familiar one answered concretely.

**Finding the content points.** They are embedded in the prompt rather than
listed. A report asking what you have achieved, what problems you met and what
training would help contains three separate requirements, and each needs its own
paragraph. Underline every instruction before starting and check each one off
before submitting.

### Genre conventions

**Essay** — formal, neutral-academic. Responds to two of the three given points,
argues a consistent position, and reaches a justified conclusion.

**Letter or email** — formal or semi-formal depending on the reader. Opens and
closes appropriately, states its purpose early, and holds one register
throughout.

**Proposal** — formal and persuasive, written forward in time for a
decision-maker. Uses section headings and ends with a clear recommendation. A
proposal that never actually proposes anything loses marks.

**Report** — formal and impersonal, written backward in time about something that
happened. Uses section headings, keeps a factual tone, and presents findings. A
report without headings reads as an essay.

**Review** — semi-formal and engaging, written for an identified readership. Must
evaluate as well as describe, and normally ends with a recommendation. Pure
description is the common failing.

### How Writing is marked

Each task is marked on four subscales — Content, Communicative Achievement,
Organisation and Language — each from 0 to 5, giving 20 marks per task.

**The four are assessed independently.** A brilliantly written answer to the
wrong question still loses Content; a well-argued answer in the wrong register
still loses Communicative Achievement. Strength on one subscale does not lift
another.

Bands 1, 3 and 5 have written descriptors. Bands 2 and 4 are awarded when a
performance shares features of the bands above and below, and band 0 is for
performance below band 1 or for an answer too short or too far off task to
assess.

Marking is criterion-referenced against the descriptors, not against other
candidates in the session. Most passing candidates score around 3 on most
subscales; a 5 is genuinely rare.

### Content

Content asks a single question: is the target reader informed?

The target reader is whoever the task invents for you to write to — a tutor, a
manager, a magazine's readership. They count as *informed* once every content
point and every requirement has been dealt with and taken as far as it needs to
go. Some points need only a statement; others ask you to describe or explain, and
those need developing.

Relevance is judged against the task, so an accurate and interesting paragraph
about something the task did not ask for does not earn Content marks and may
crowd out something that would.

This is the subscale most often lost through carelessness rather than weak
English: a buried second instruction, a comparison not made, a third point
discussed when two were asked for.

### Communicative Achievement

Communicative Achievement asks whether the answer uses the conventions of its
task to reach its reader.

Conventions cover genre, format, register and function: a personal letter should
not be laid out as a formal report, and a proposal should recommend rather than
merely describe. Function means what the task asked the writing to *do* — make a
complaint, suggest alternatives, persuade.

Holding the reader's attention is meant positively: the text lets the reader take
meaning from it without being distracted. It does not mean a text that forces
close reading because it is hard to follow.

Higher bands also require handling both straightforward and complex ideas as the
task demands. Straightforward ideas concern limited, usually concrete subject
matter; complex ideas are more abstract and need more from the writer to express
clearly.

The commonest failure is drift: a proposal that opens formally and slides into
conversational language by the third paragraph.

### Organisation

Organisation covers how the text is put together and held together.

Three things are distinguished. **Linking words** are the explicit, high-frequency
connectors — *and*, *but*, *because*, *first of all*, *finally*. **Cohesive
devices** are more sophisticated: phrases such as *moreover* or *as a result*,
and grammatical devices including reference pronouns, substitution and ellipsis.
**Organisational patterns** are the less explicit ways of connecting beyond the
sentence — arranging points in climactic order, parallelism, using a question to
open a paragraph.

Range and control both count, and range is what separates the top bands. An
answer that repeats the same three linkers throughout is unlikely to go above the
middle of the scale, however correct they are. Referencing back with a
demonstrative and a summarising noun — *this shift*, *such concerns* — links and
interprets at the same time, and is under-used.

Paragraphing is part of organisation: one idea per paragraph, signalled early.

### Language

Language covers vocabulary and grammar, and is judged on range first, then
control.

Vocabulary is graded from basic, through everyday, to **less common lexis** —
items that appear less often and let an idea be expressed more precisely.
Appropriacy matters as much as range: a word must fit its context and its
collocations. *Sensible* where *sensitive* is meant is an appropriacy error, and
so is *big snow* where English says *heavy snow*.

Grammar is judged on the range of simple and complex forms and on the control of
them. An answer with no errors but only simple structures does not reach the top
bands: range is part of the criterion, not a bonus.

Errors are weighed by their effect. Slips and errors in less common words and
structures are tolerated at the higher bands; errors that impede communication
cap the subscale low regardless of how ambitious the language is.

## Listening and Speaking

These two papers are outside what this application covers, and are recorded here
only so the shape of the whole examination is clear.

**Listening** takes about 40 minutes and has four parts with 30 questions. Every
recording is heard twice. The parts cover short extracts with multiple-choice
questions, a monologue with sentence completion, an extended conversation with
multiple-choice questions, and a final part with two matching tasks over a series
of short monologues.

**Speaking** takes about 15 minutes and has four parts, normally with two
candidates and two examiners: one interlocutor who manages the test, one assessor
who only observes and marks. The parts are a short interview, an individual long
turn comparing pictures, a collaborative task between the candidates, and a
discussion developing that theme. Performance is marked on grammar and
vocabulary, discourse management, pronunciation and interactive communication,
with a separate global mark.
